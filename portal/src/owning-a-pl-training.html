Owninga Profitand Loss

Run your department like a business you own. Revenue, cost, margin — and the trade-offs between them are yours to make.

Why this matters

A department is a business inside a business.

The Profit and Loss statement — the P&L — is the scoreboard for that business. It shows what came in, what it cost to deliver, and what was left. Own the P&L and you stop managing tasks; you start managing an outcome.

What success looks like
You're winning when…
You can read your P&L top to bottomrevenue, cost of delivery, margin, profit — no gaps
You know your unit economics coldwhat a customer costs to win vs what they're worth
You make trade-offs on purposeinvest or protect margin — a choice, not an accident
You never plan spend you can't fundcash and runway checked before the commitment

This is the bar. Everything that follows is how you clear it — and the final gate checks you against it.

The distinction that decides everything
Revenue is what you bill. Profit is what you keep.

Revenue is vanity. Profit is sanity. A department can grow revenue every month and still lose money — because revenue says nothing about what it cost to earn. The number that matters is what's left after every cost comes out.

Growth without margin is just expensive noise. Chase the number you keep, not the number you announce.

The line most owners skip · don't
Gross margin is the truth about delivery.

Gross margin is revenue minus the cost of actually producing the work — before overhead. It tells you whether the thing you sell is even worth selling. A department with thin gross margin can never operate its way to profit: the leak is in the product, not the admin.

Fix margin at the source. If it costs S$90 of delivery to earn S$100 of revenue, no amount of cost-cutting elsewhere saves it.
Part two

Unit economics

The health of one customer, before you scale to a thousand.

The value side
Lifetime Value — what a customer is worth.

Lifetime Value (LTV) is the total gross margin one customer brings across the whole time they stay with you — not their first invoice, their whole relationship. Longer retention and higher margin per month both push it up. LTV is the size of the prize for winning one customer.

The cost side
Customer Acquisition Cost — what a customer costs to win.

Customer Acquisition Cost (CAC) is everything you spend to land one new customer — ads, content, sales time, tooling — divided by the number of customers it won. If you spend S$5,000 in a month and win 10 customers, your CAC is S$500. It is the price of the ticket to play.

3:1 is the working floor. Below it, growth burns cash. Well above it, you're likely under-investing — there's room to spend more to win faster.
Know which clients and products actually pay
Contribution margin — per client, per product.

Contribution margin is what a single client or product leaves after its own direct costs — the margin it contributes to covering your overhead. Averages hide the truth: some clients subsidise the business, others quietly drain it.

Contributes →

  • Revenue comfortably above its delivery cost
  • Retains — value compounds over time
  • Low to serve, high to keep

Drains ✕

  • Heavy to deliver, barely above cost
  • High-maintenance, churns fast
  • Looks like revenue, lands as a loss

Rank your clients and products by contribution. Double down on the top; fix, reprice, or exit the bottom.

The owner's trade-offs

Where the real job is — choosing, not just reporting.

Capital allocation · cash gate first

Cash buys the right to invest. Economics decide where.

Gate 1 · CashCan we fund the full bet?

Runway and the reserve survive even if the return arrives late.

→

Part four

Your turn

Three calls an owner has to make. Get them graded.

Question 1 · revenue vs profit
Your department billed S$120,000 this month, up 30%. After cost of delivery and operating costs, it kept S$4,000.
What is the real health signal here?
Question 2 · unit economics
You spend S$500 to win a client (Customer Acquisition Cost) who's worth S$900 in lifetime margin (Lifetime Value).
Is this healthy enough to scale the spend?
Question 3 · cash & the trade-off
A promising growth bet needs S$20,000 of spend over three months. Your department's funded budget covers S$8,000.
As the P&L owner, what do you do?
