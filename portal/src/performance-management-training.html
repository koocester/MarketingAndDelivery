Performancemanagement & theImprovement Program

The fair, consistent process Human Resources (HR) owns to handle under-performance with dignity — the formal step after a manager's coaching hasn't closed the gap.

What success looks like
You're winning when…
Under-performance is caught earlyaddressed while it's still fixable, not months late
Most people recovera clear program pulls the majority back on track
No one is ever blindsidedexpectations were explicit long before any decision
The process is identical for everyonesame steps, no favouritism, no surprises
Every step is documentedfair to the person, protective of the company
Dignity is never optionaleven an exit is handled with respect

This is the bar. Everything that follows is how you hit it — and the final gate checks you against it.

HR's job here
Make it fair, consistent, documented, and humane.

A good process protects both sides — the person gets a real, honest chance to recover, and the company gets a defensible record. That only holds if you run it the same way every time: no surprises, no favouritism.

Part one

The conversation & the record

Before any program, how you talk and what you write down.

The performance conversation

Direct, specific, evidence-based — and kind.

Use the numbers. Name the gap plainly, tie it to what was expected, and stay warm while you do it. The goal is a person who leaves clear on what must change and believing you're on their side.

Separate the behaviour

  • "The last three deliverables missed their deadline by a week"
  • Facts, dates, numbers — not adjectives
  • Tied to a clear, agreed expectation
  • Opens a path: "here's what good looks like"

Not the person

  • "You're careless / lazy / not a team player"
  • Labels, character, tone-policing
  • Vague — "your attitude needs work"
  • Closes the door and invites defensiveness

Attack the gap, never the human. A person can change behaviour — they can't change a label you've pinned on them.

Documentation · why it matters, done simply

If it isn't written down, it didn't happen.

  • It's fairness — a written record means the person is judged on facts, not on someone's memory or mood.
  • It's protection — for them and for the company, if the decision is ever questioned.
  • Keep it simple — date, what was discussed, what was agreed, by when. A few honest lines after each conversation beats a perfect document written after the fact.
  • Write it near the moment — same day, while it's accurate. Late notes look like a case being built.
The rule: document as you go, in plain language. The record should read as a fair account anyone could pick up — not a paper trail assembled to justify a decision already made.
Part two

The Performance Improvement Program

The core of this module — abbreviated PIP.

The Performance Improvement Program (PIP) · what it must always include

Four things. Never fewer.

Miss one and it isn't a fair PIP. No support means you set them up to fail; no clear success bar means the outcome is an opinion, not a fact.
The timeline · a real window, with support inside it

30–60 days. Check in the whole way through.

Day 0 · Kickoff
expectations, support & success bar agreed in writing
→check-in
Week 2
early progress, adjust support
→check-in
Week 4
honest midpoint read
→
Day 30–60 · Review
measured against the bar
The check-ins are the point. Regular reviews mean the person always knows where they stand — the final review confirms what the check-ins already showed. No one hears the outcome for the first time on the last day.
Inside the program · the bar must be unambiguous
Define "success" so both of you would score it the same.

A vague target — "improve your work" — is unfair; no one can pass it or fail it cleanly. Write the bar as something measurable and dated: the deliverables, the quality, the deadline they must hit by the end of the window. If two people would disagree on whether it was met, it isn't defined yet.

The two outcomes · dignity is non-negotiable either way

Passes, or doesn't. Both handled with respect.

Passes →

  • Back on track — the gap is closed against the bar
  • Recognised, not held under suspicion afterwards
  • Return to the normal performance cadence
  • The program worked exactly as intended

Does not pass →

  • A respectful role change, or a respectful exit
  • No blindsiding — the check-ins made it foreseeable
  • Handled privately, with notice and support
  • They leave with their dignity intact
Dignity is not the reward for passing. It's how every person is treated through the whole process — including the one who has to leave.
How it connects · not a standalone event

One piece of a bigger system.

  • Sits on the Rewards & consequences ladder — coaching and feedback come before it; a role change or exit comes after. The Program is the fair, formal middle step.
  • Feeds from the bi-annual evaluation — a low rating is often what surfaces a real gap and triggers the conversation. The evaluation flags it; the Program addresses it.
  • Feeds back into it — a passed program shows up as recovery in the next evaluation; a defined success bar makes that judgment easy and fair.
The through-line: evaluation surfaces the gap → conversation names it → the Program gives a fair, supported chance to close it → the outcome is recorded, with dignity, either way.

Certification · answer Yes to every question

Can you run a fair Performance Improvement Program with dignity?

Do you reach the Program only after coaching and feedback haven't closed the gap?
Do you hold the conversation with evidence, separating the behaviour from the person?
Do you document simply, in plain language, near the moment?
Does every Program include expectations, support, a timeline, and a defined success bar?

Handle it early, run it fairly, protect the person's dignity.

The Performance Improvement Program isn't a trap — it's a real, supported chance to recover, run the same way for everyone. Most people come back through it. The ones who don't leave with respect. Either way, no one is ever blindsided.
