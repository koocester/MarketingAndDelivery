Two things that look alike · tell them apart

A bet

you commit resources to it
  • You can name what it costs and what it returns
  • Your best people are actually assigned to it
  • You would defend it if budget got cut in half

A wish

it sits on a list, unfunded
  • It sounds good but nobody owns it
  • No people, no budget, no deadline behind it
  • It survives only because cutting it feels like loss

A plan full of wishes feels productive and delivers nothing. Fund the bets. Retire the wishes honestly.

Part one

Own the numbers

Lead versus lag, and the one metric that matters.

How you read your department

Lag tells you what happened. Lead tells you what's coming.

Lead indicators

inputs you can act on today
  • Calls booked, drafts shipped, shoots scheduled
  • You can change them this week
  • Manage these — they produce the outcome

Lag indicators

outcomes you can only watch
  • Revenue, qualified leads, retention
  • By the time they move, the month is set
  • Watch these — they validate the leads
You cannot push a lag number directly. You push the lead measures that feed it, then watch the lag to confirm the leads were the right ones.

Part two

Put resources on the bets

People and budget are scarce. Spend them like it.

Every yes is a no to something else
Do your best people and budget sit on the bets — with a defended “not now” list?
PeopleName the strongest owners

Do not split your best performers across five priorities.

BudgetShow the actual allocation

Every funded yes names what receives less money or attention.

Not nowPublish and defend the list

New requests wait unless leadership explicitly changes the strategy.

If the answer is no, the strategy is theatre. Keep a reserve, then concentrate everything else on the few bets.
The discipline that makes a strategy real
A strategy is what you don't do.

Anyone can add. Strategy is subtraction — the good ideas you deliberately refuse so the great one gets everything it needs. Keep a "not now" list next to your bets and defend it. The department watches what you say no to far more closely than what you say yes to.

Before you commit the department · stress-test the bet

Five questions before people and budget move.

01 · ConstraintDoes it unlock the system?

Attack the limiting point, not a local inconvenience.

02 · FailureWhat guarantees it fails?

Name the conditions already working against the bet.

03 · RippleAnd then what?

Trace the second and third consequences.

04 · Base rateWhat happened last time?

Use actual time, cost and conversion from a similar bet.

05 · ReversibleOne-way or two-way door?

Move fast when cheap to reverse; slow down when costly.

Commit only when the bet survives all five. The filter protects the department from confident guesses.

Reviewing bets · when to double down, when to kill

Run the direction test on every bet.

Double down →

  • The lead measures are being hit
  • The lag number is starting to follow
  • If it were running perfectly, the direction is still right
  • You'd start this bet again today knowing what you know

Kill it ✕

  • Leads are hit but the outcome never moves
  • The direction is wrong, not just the execution
  • It survives only because stopping feels like failure
  • It's draining people the winning bets need

Killing a wrong bet isn't a failure of commitment — staying in one is. Reallocate the freed people and budget to what's working. Do it early; a dead bet gets more expensive every week you keep it alive.
