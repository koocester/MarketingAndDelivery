Device &accessadministration

The admin counterpart to the staff modules. Everyone else looks after their own device and account — you provision, secure and de-provision devices and access for the whole company.

Who this is for

You hold the master keys.

Information Technology (IT) owns the fleet and the access map. Staff protect the device and login in front of them; you set up every device, grant every login, and take them all back when people leave. If it's a company laptop, a shared vault, or a service key — it's yours to administer.

This is the admin side. Staff-facing modules teach people to look after their own device and account. This module is how you run it for everyone.

Part one

The device fleet

Provision it, secure it, keep it wipeable.

Provisioning · a device isn't ready until it's enrolled
1Enrol before you hand it over
A new laptop is not "set up" until it is enrolled in Mobile Device Management (MDM), access-controlled and recorded in the inventory. No exceptions.
2 Enrol in Mobile Device Management (MDM) 3 Apply the security baseline 4 Install approved software only 5 Record it in the device inventory

Mobile Device Management (MDM) is the console that lets you push settings, patches and — critically — a remote wipe to every device you manage. If it isn't enrolled, you can't protect it or recover it.

Keep the fleet current · centrally, not device by device

Patch and enforce from one console.

  • Push updates centrally — operating system and app patches roll out from Mobile Device Management (MDM), so no device is left running old, exploitable software.
  • Enforce a security baseline — screen-lock timeout, firewall up, approved software only and no disabling protections. Set it once, apply it to every device.
  • Watch for drift — a device that falls out of policy, such as updates paused or screen lock disabled, is flagged and brought back into line.
Most breaches exploit unpatched software. Central patching is the single highest-leverage thing you do for the fleet — one push protects everyone.
Lost, stolen, or leaving
If a device is gone, you wipe it remotely.

A reported-lost or stolen laptop, and every device at offboarding, gets wiped from Mobile Device Management (MDM). Cut its access first, then wipe — the faster you act, the less exposure.

This rule belongs to the whole team. Everyone reports a lost or stolen device immediately. Information Technology cuts access and wipes it. A device that was never enrolled cannot be wiped, so enrolment remains non-negotiable.
Part two

The access lifecycle

Joiner → mover → leaver. Grant, adjust, revoke.

Every person moves through three states

Grant it, adjust it, take it all back.

Joiner
Grant after sign-off
→
Mover
Adjust on role change
→
Leaver
Revoke everything
  • Joiner — grant access only after the IT & Digital Access Declaration is signed, and only what the role needs.
  • Mover — on a role change, add what the new role needs and remove what the old one did. Access should never just accumulate.
  • Leaver — on the last day, revoke everything and wipe the device. No lingering logins.
Granting access · the two rules

Declaration first

no signature, no logins
  • The signed IT & Digital Access Declaration comes before any access
  • It's the switch that turns access on — you don't grant ahead of it
  • No signed form? Access waits.

Least privilege

only what the role needs
  • Grant the minimum access the role requires — nothing "just in case"
  • Every extra login is another door an attacker could use
  • Scope tightly; expand only on a real, stated need
The leaver · your most important admin task
An ex-employee with a live login is a serious risk.

When someone leaves, revoke every access promptly — email, tools, shared vaults, third-party apps, building and device access. A single forgotten login is a standing hole in the company. Do it on the last day, work from a checklist, and confirm nothing was missed.

Promptly means same day, not "when I get to it". The window between someone leaving and their access being cut is exactly when the damage happens.
Part three

Credentials & secrets

The vault, two-factor, and where keys live.

Running the password manager · the admin side

You own the vault, not just a login in it.

  • Scope shared vaults by team — each team sees only its own credentials. Who can access what is a decision you make and review, not a default.
  • Enforce two-factor authentication (2FA) everywhere — on every account that supports it, no opt-outs.
  • Never share credentials in plain text — not in Lark, chat, or email. Credentials move through the vault's share feature, never pasted into a message.
  • Rotate on staff changes — when someone with access to a shared credential leaves or moves, change it. Their departure shouldn't leave a known password behind.
Secrets · Application Programming Interface (API) keys and service credentials
Secrets live in a store — never hard-coded.

Application Programming Interface (API) keys, database passwords and service credentials belong in a secrets store, referenced by the systems that need them. They are never pasted inline into a workflow, script, or config. If a secret has ever been exposed in plain text — in code, a message, or a screenshot — treat it as compromised and rotate it.

Ties to the n8n rule: any secret found hard-coded inside an automation gets moved to the store and rotated. An exposed key is a live key until you change it.
Part four

Audit & change safely

Know who has what. Change it on the record.

Access auditing · keep the map current

Know who has access to what — and review it.

  • Keep a live picture — maintain a record of who holds which access, across every system. If you can't see it, you can't secure it.
  • Review on a schedule — walk the map regularly and ask, for each entry, "does this person still need this?"
  • Remove stale access — dormant logins, access left over from an old role, accounts nobody uses. Every one you remove is one less way in.
Access creep is the quiet risk. People gain access over time and rarely lose it. Regular review is what keeps least-privilege true months later.
Change safely · leave a trail

Do this →

  • Document every access or config change — who, what, when, why
  • Make changes deliberately, one at a time, so you can undo them
  • Perform your own changes — never let someone else make them on your behalf
  • Report and act on any suspected compromise immediately
  • Cut access first, investigate second — contain before you diagnose

Never ✕

  • Silent changes nobody can trace back later
  • Granting access "temporarily" with no plan to remove it
  • Handing your change to another person to perform under your responsibility
  • Sitting on a suspected breach to avoid raising an alarm
  • Sharing a credential in plain text to move fast

There's no blame for reporting a suspected compromise early. There is a real cost to hiding one.
