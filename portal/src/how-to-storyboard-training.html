Part one

The arc

A video is not a list of scenes. It's a chain — each stage earns the next.

What a storyboard is · and why it matters
A great arc makes people watch to the end — and act.

Storyboard it before you shoot it. Every stage has its own pass/fail questions. If a stage can't answer them on paper, it won't answer them on camera either — fix it in the plan, not in the edit.
This isn't just for producers. Every Koocester video runs through this exact chain — it's the shared language marketing, strategists, editors, and CS all speak when they talk about a video. Know the arc, and you can read, brief, or critique any video in the pipeline.

Before you plan · what to gather from the guest, in the call

Five things to pull from every guest — mark the musts.

Must gather

no storyboard without these
  • Story & credibility — their journey, the turning points
  • The numbers — revenue, costs, timelines — the stuff that lands as story
  • Dream outcome — the win they deliver to a viewer
  • Offer for the CTA — paid: the client's offer · unpaid: our lead-gen offer

Good to have

shapes the plan, not a blocker
  • Winning picture — what success looks like to them. Set expectations, don't overpromise.
  • Outfit cluster — set before the shoot, not on the day.
Get this in the call, before you plan. Everything else in this deck builds on these five things.
Paid vs unpaid · plan every video, by default

Every video gets planned. By default, every video is planned as unpaid — objective is our lead generation, and you don't need the guest present to plan it. Gather what you need from them first (previous slide), then storyboard solo.

Unpaid

organic · the default — plan it yourself
  • You storyboard it yourself — the guest doesn't need to be present
  • Objective is always our lead generation
  • DO NOT show the template
  • Set expectations clearly — never overpromise

Paid

client-funded
  • Storyboard it WITH the interviewee, together
  • Client controls the objective
  • Show them the template
  • Set expectations clearly — never overpromise

Part two

Design the questions and the shot

Five rules for questions. One formula for every visual.

Question Design SOP · 5 rules
  1. One ask per question — never stack two questions in one.
  2. Max ten words — long questions get long, wandering answers.
  3. Specific over abstract — "This Ferrari" beats "your car."
  4. Force topic-first answers — the single highest-leverage edit you can make to a question.
  5. Coach the guest's tone — vary pace, let the number land, vocal smile, avoid monotone, conversational not presentational.
★ the core skill
Visual prompting formula · every shot on the storyboard
This is the skill that matters most. If you can't picture the shot, you can't storyboard it — every single item on this storyboard must resolve to a visual prompt.
[person] + [action] + [environment] + [emotional state] + [framing]
Production rule. A visual prompt must include [person] + [action] at minimum — an empty environment with no one doing anything is not a shot.
Part three

Build the arc, stage by stage

Hook. Establishment. Four arcs. Resolve. Trust. Each with its own pass/fail bar.

1Hookmost important scene

Suggest 3 hooks per video. Each must pass ALL three tests, or it's rejected. This is the single highest-leverage scene in the video.

Example. "What do you do for a living?" → "I own Snoopy IP" → hook = an overview of the Snoopy Truck as the interviewee steps out; title card: "1 of 1 Snoopy Truck in Singapore." Visual prompt: [interviewee] + [steps out of the 1-of-1 Snoopy Truck] + [driveway] + [excited, proud] + [wide reveal shot].
2Establishment

Passes when it earns the next 30 seconds — never by stating credentials.

Rule. Pull authority through a lesson, an insight, or a story — NEVER a raw stat or self-praise. Two worked examples, with the exact shot to visualize, are on the next slide.
Establishment in practice · see the shot before you shoot it

Same rule, two worked examples.

$250k renovation

"What's the BEST investment in that?!" turns the number into a story, not a flex.

Visual prompt: [interviewee] + [walks through, gesturing at a detail] + [the renovated room] + [proud, animated] + [medium tracking shot]

$10M revenue, up from $2M

"When you crossed $10M, what changed about how you run the business?" — educational, not a flex.

Visual prompt: [interviewee] + [sits back, reflecting] + [office desk] + [thoughtful] + [close-up]

Picture it on the field. Authority comes through the story — and the story only lands if you can already see the shot before you call action.
3Arc 1 · People first

Must tie back to the Hook and forward to the CTA.

Example. Theme of the house — Japanese × Middle-east. "Offices are emotionally dead — Snoopy brings emotion back."
4Arc 2 · Value of the tour

The money-worth beat — makes the tour feel super valuable.

Example. The curves + open-kitchen must-have. "30 full-timers, ~$100k monthly overheads" — heartfelt, come-a-long-way, not a brag.
5Arc 3 · Mid CTA

Must feel natural — never a bolted-on ad break.

Example. Giveaway of a height-adjustable table + ergonomic chair + a 1-of-300 limited Snoopy collectible. "What do they comment to get this?" → "COMMENT SNOOPY!"
6Arc 4 · Celebratory scene

Short. A real feeling, not a montage for its own sake.

Example. Giant beanbags, lockers, a meeting pod. "A space for everyone in the family."
7Resolve

Settles the video — closes the loop the Hook opened.

Example. "Stopped being the bottleneck. Built systems that ran without me." → the CRM / PM / SOP six-months-of-unsexy-work story → lesson: "most owners think they have a revenue problem; usually it's a systems problem. Build the machine, then let it run."
8Trust · Application + Ending CTA

Application = a real-life implementation viewers can try themselves.

Tier 1 directly owns the revenue — the CTA IS the transaction Tier 2 high-intent leads to convert fast Tier 3 indirect — nurture later
Core rule — client success. For paid videos, the win is the client's fulfilment and great word of mouth. Storyboard toward the client's revenue / success, always.
Examples. "Comment FOUNDER for 50% off the next Founder event." · "Comment RENOVATE — first 100 free slots + a chance at $10,000 off."
Part four

Ship it

Final checks, production rules, compliance, and the mindset that runs it all.

Final checks · before it's a real storyboard

Production

  • Visual prompt has [person] + [action] minimum
  • Short, confident questions
  • Flag anything that needs visualisation
  • Sincere conversation, not a presentation

Compliance — never

  • Unverifiable claims — no "best in Singapore"
  • Unclear music or footage IP
  • Sensitive content left unflagged
The most important storyboarding skill · not a footnote
How to visualize the shot.

Every stage, every question, every CTA — none of it matters if you can't see it before you shoot it. This is the skill.

  1. Picture the space first. Read the location before you plan a single beat — where does the person stand, where do they move, what's behind them?
  2. See the A-roll and the B-roll. For every beat: A-roll = [person] + [action] + [emotion] + [framing]. B-roll = the cutaway that proves it's true.
  3. Run the whole video in your head. Start to finish — does each scene bridge to the next? No dead air, no gap you can't picture.
  4. Write it so a stranger could shoot it. If someone else read your visual prompt with zero context, could they get the shot right, without you on set?
  5. Ask what the viewer would shout at the screen. Then make sure the shot answers it — not just the words.
Worked example. Answer: "We stopped taking on every client — now we're selective." A-roll: [interviewee] + [shakes head, half-smiles recalling it] + [office, seated] + [reflective, a little rueful] + [close-up]. B-roll: [hand] + [flips through a thick stack of old client files] + [desk] + [busy, overwhelmed energy] + [overhead shot] — proves the "before," without a single stat on screen.
